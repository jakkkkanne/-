Doing Business in Japanビジネスマナー

The unwritten rules your Japanese partners will notice — on one page each. Print it, read it on the flight, and walk into your first meeting with confidence.

01
Be 5 minutes early — not on time.Arriving exactly on time is considered slightly late.
02
Bring 2–3× more business cards than you think.Everyone in the room exchanges cards with everyone.
03
Treat a business card like the person.Never write on it, fold it, or put it in your back pocket.
04
Wait to be seated.Seats have a rank. Your host will guide you (see page 3).
05
Silence is not a problem.Pauses mean careful thinking. Don't rush to fill them.
06
"We will consider it" often means no.Decisions are made later, by consensus — don't push for a yes in the room.
07
Never pour your own drink.Pour for others; they will pour for you.
08
Bring a small gift (omiyage).Something from your city, nicely wrapped (page 4).
09
Don't tip.Not in restaurants, taxis or hotels. It can cause confusion.
10
Send a thank-you email the same day.Templates in Japanese & English on page 4.

Business Cards & Bowing名刺交換・お辞儀

The card exchange (meishi kōkan) is the first impression. It takes 10 seconds and is watched closely.

The card exchange, step by step 名刺交換

  1. Stand up. Never exchange cards seated or across a table — walk around it.
  2. Hold your card with both hands, text facing the other person, at chest height.
  3. The visitor offers first, and the junior person offers first. Say your company and name, then "Yoroshiku onegaishimasu."
  4. Hold your card slightly lower than theirs — a small sign of respect.
  1. Receive with both hands and say "Chōdai itashimasu" (I humbly receive it).
  2. Read it for a moment. Check the name and title — it shows interest.
  3. Place it on the table, on top of your card case, for the whole meeting. With several people, line the cards up in their seating order.
  4. Put it away carefully when the meeting ends — into a card case, never a pocket.
Writing notes on someone's card, playing with it, or leaving it behind on the table.
Pulling a card from your wallet or back pocket. Use a card case.

Three bows to know お辞儀

Eshaku 会釈
Light bow. Passing someone in the hallway, entering a room.
Keirei 敬礼
Standard bow. Greeting clients, card exchange, saying thank you.
Saikeirei 最敬礼
Deep bow. Sincere apology or deep gratitude. Rare for visitors.
Bow from the waist with a straight back; eyes look down, not at the other person.
As a foreign visitor, a handshake is often offered — accept it, and add a small nod.
When in doubt, match the depth of the other person's bow.

Seating Order & Dinners上座・会食

Every room has a "top seat" (kamiza 上座) — the one farthest from the door. Guests and seniors sit there. The seat nearest the door (shimoza 下座) is for the most junior host.

Meeting room 会議室

Taxi タクシー

Seat 1 is behind the driver. The front passenger seat is the lowest — the junior host sits there to pay and guide. Exception: if your host drives their own car, the front seat next to them is the top seat.

At the dinner table 会食・飲み会

Wait for the host's "Kanpai!" before drinking anything.
When clinking glasses with a senior person, hold yours slightly lower.
Watch others' glasses and refill them. Hold the bottle with both hands.
When someone pours for you, lift your glass with both hands.
Want to stop drinking? Leave your glass full.
Pouring your own drink.
Sticking chopsticks upright in rice, or passing food chopstick-to-chopstick (both are funeral customs).
Blowing your nose at the table.
Fighting over the bill. If invited, the host pays — thank them the next day.
Not sure where to sit? Stand near the door and wait. Your host will gesture and say 「どうぞ、こちらへ」 Dōzo, kochira e — "Please, this way." Accept once; if you're offered the top seat, it's polite to sit there.

Gifts & Thank-You Emails手土産・お礼メール

Choosing an omiyage 手土産

Local & edible: chocolates, cookies, maple syrup, coffee — something from your city or state.
Individually wrapped pieces, so it can be shared around the office.
$20–50 for a team gift. Bring an extra one for unexpected meetings.
Give it at the end of the meeting, with both hands, out of its shopping bag.
Sets of 4 or 9 items — the words sound like "death" and "suffering".
Knives or scissors — they suggest "cutting ties".
Very expensive gifts — they create an obligation to give back.
Black-and-white ribbons or wrapping (used for funerals).
Say: 「ささやかですが、どうぞ」 Sasayaka desu ga, dōzo — "It's just a small something, please."

Same-day thank-you email お礼メール

Copy, replace the [brackets], and send. Japanese partners will appreciate the effort, even if they reply in English.

件名：本日のお打ち合わせのお礼Subject: Thank you for today's meeting [会社名] [部署名]
[相手の名前] 様[Company] [Department] — Mr./Ms. [Name] ("sama" = respectful title) いつも大変お世話になっております。
[自分の会社名]の[自分の名前]です。Thank you for your continued support. This is [your name] from [your company]. 本日はお忙しい中、貴重なお時間をいただき、誠にありがとうございました。Thank you very much for taking valuable time out of your busy schedule today. [話し合った内容]について、引き続き検討を進めてまいります。We will continue to move forward on [topic we discussed]. 今後ともどうぞよろしくお願いいたします。We look forward to continuing to work with you.

After you fly home 帰国後

Send the thank-you email within 24 hours.
Note each person's name, title and seat from the cards you collected.
Share any photos taken together.
Next visit, open with: 「先日はありがとうございました」 Senjitsu wa arigatō gozaimashita — "Thank you for the other day."
